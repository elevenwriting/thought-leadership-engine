# Playbooks

How to use the thought leadership database for each job. Every playbook follows the
same ground rules:

- **Cite everything.** Each item has a `citation` (e.g. `J SMITH - SLACK #NEWS - 19.09.2026`).
  Attach it to every claim, quote or idea you take from an item.
- **Respect visibility.** Items with a `private_flag` came from internal sources. Use them
  to inform angles, arguments and ideas, but don't quote them, name the meeting or channel,
  or present them as publishable without the author's approval. Say clearly which parts of
  your output rest on private items. Public items can be quoted.
- **Read before you write.** Search results carry summaries. Before relying on an item, fetch
  it with its full text: summaries lose nuance, and the strongest material is often a
  passing remark the summary left out.
- **Keep their voice.** Use the person's own phrasing and strong opinions. Don't soften them.
- **Say what's missing.** If the database doesn't have enough on a topic, say so and suggest
  who to ask (see the people and what they're responsible for in the taxonomy).

## query

**"What's our stance on X?" / "I'm writing about X."**

1. Get the taxonomy. Map X to the closest themes.
2. Search by those themes, and separately by a short text phrase for X. Tags give precision,
   text catches what tags missed.
3. Fetch the strongest 5 to 10 items in full.
4. Answer with the company's position, the arguments behind it, where people disagree,
   and the best quotable lines, each with its citation and visibility.

## coverage

**"What should we make next?" / "Where are our gaps?"**

1. Get coverage.
2. Report three lists: theme x narrative cells with material but no content made yet
   (quickest wins), cells with no material at all (who should we interview?), and formats
   never used for strong cells.
3. Turn the top gaps into a draft content calendar: one line per piece, with theme,
   narrative, format, lead author and the items it would draw on.

## updates

**"Is our existing content out of date?"**

1. Ask for the existing piece (URL or text) or its topic.
2. Search the same themes for items newer than the piece's publish date.
3. List new insights, changed opinions, fresh data or examples that the piece should
   reflect, each with its citation, and suggest specific edits.

## trends

**"What are people talking about?"**

1. Get trends (default 30 days; try 7 and 90 too).
2. Report the themes, narratives and authors rising fastest, and anything that's gone quiet.
3. For the top two or three rising themes, search and read the recent items, then say
   what the conversation actually is, not just that it's busy.

## atomise

**"Turn this into posts."**

1. Fetch the long item (a transcript, document or interview) in full.
2. Pull out every distinct, self-contained idea: an opinion, a story, a number, a framework.
3. Draft one short post per idea in the author's voice, each tagged with its narrative.
   For private items, draft them as suggestions for the author to approve.

## aggregate

**"What's the full story on X over the last few months?"**

1. Search by theme and date range; read the items in date order.
2. Write the narrative arc: where the thinking started, what changed it, where it is now.
3. Cite each step. Flag contradictions rather than smoothing them over.

## case-studies

**"Turn client work into a case study."**

1. Search for the client or project name across meetings, Slack and email.
2. Build the case study structure: situation, problem, what we did, results (with numbers),
   what we learned. Note which facts come from private items.
3. List what's missing (usually hard numbers and client sign-off) and who can provide it.

## mining

**"Find trends in our own data."**

1. Search for items tagged Data storytelling or containing numbers, percentages or results.
2. Group the findings, relate each to a theme and narrative, and suggest the story each one tells.

## debate

**"Where do we disagree?"**

1. For a theme, read items from several different authors.
2. Find points where people take different positions, or where someone argues against
   the industry view.
3. Frame each as a provocative piece: the question, both sides with citations, and which
   side the company could take publicly. Internal disagreement from private items needs
   both people's approval before publishing.

## foresight

**"When did we call it early?"**

1. Search for items tagged Predictions, and older items whose themes are trending now.
2. Match each prediction against what has since happened (ask the user or use what you know).
3. Draft "we said this in [month]" content, citing the dated original.

## post-mortems

**"Show how we learn."**

1. Search for items tagged Behind the scenes, and for words like "mistake", "lesson",
   "changed", "didn't work", "retro".
2. Build a before → what happened → what we do now story, with citations.

## briefs

**"Write a content brief for X."**

1. Run the query playbook for X.
2. Produce a brief: working title, angle (narrative), audience, key argument, outline,
   quotes to use (public) and points to paraphrase (private), data, suggested graphics,
   the author to byline or interview, and every citation.

## media

**"Give me ideas for podcasts, video, infographics."**

1. Use coverage and trends to find strong themes.
2. For each format, suggest specific episodes, videos or graphics: the hook, the guest or
   presenter (from the people list) and the items that supply the material.

## after-publishing

When something is made from an item, record it as a deliverable (`Format: title or URL`)
so coverage stays accurate and the item is marked as used.
